## 2. Policy Landscape

Any discussion of battery interconnection policy in 2026 has to start from a piece of federal action that, at first glance, looks like it should already have solved the problem Section 1 described, and did not: Federal Order 2222-B. The order was issued in 2024 [S1], with compliance filings from regional transmission organizations (RTOs) required through 2026 [S1], and it requires each RTO to accept aggregations of distributed resources, including distributed solar, behind-the-meter batteries, and controllable water heaters, as a single dispatchable resource in wholesale markets, provided the aggregator can demonstrate device-level telemetry [S1]. Read quickly, that sounds like exactly the kind of federal push that should speed up interconnection everywhere. Read carefully, it is almost entirely beside the point of the queue delays described in Section 1, and understanding why is the purpose of this section.

The order governs one thing only: what happens after a distributed energy resource is already interconnected and wants to sell into a wholesale market run by an RTO. It does not touch retail interconnection rules at all [S1]. A state's distribution utility still decides, on its own authority and under its own state's procedures, how an interconnection application is processed, which queue it sits in, what triggers a supplemental engineering review, and who pays for any upgrade that review finds necessary. None of that changed when Federal Order 2222-B took effect, and nothing in this report should be read as proposing that it should; the order and the retail process described in Section 1 are two different regulatory tracks that happen to both involve the same battery.

That said, the order has produced two secondary effects worth naming plainly, because both bear on why state-level reform matters more, not less, now that the order exists. First, utilities report that Federal Order 2222-B compliance filings have pulled engineering staff away from retail interconnection review, an effect utilities themselves estimate has lengthened queues by roughly 15 percent [S1] in the twelve months following a utility's compliance filing, though that figure is self-reported by the utilities and has not been independently audited by any state or federal body. If accurate even approximately, it means the same limited pool of interconnection engineers Section 1 described as the eighteen states' most commonly cited bottleneck is now also absorbing a new federal compliance workload, on top of, not instead of, retail application review.

Second, and less obviously, the order has given aggregators a new reason to push for faster retail interconnection than they had before. A distributed resource that is stuck in a retail interconnection queue cannot yet participate in the wholesale-market aggregation Federal Order 2222-B enables, which means every day a battery spends waiting for permission-to-operate is now also a day of lost wholesale-market revenue for whichever aggregator was counting on that resource. That creates commercial pressure on state processes that did not exist, at this scale, before the order was issued, and several of the eighteen surveyed states in Section 1 are now hearing from aggregators in a way they were not two years ago.

It is worth dwelling briefly on the device-level telemetry requirement itself, because it explains why the order's effects reach retail interconnection at all despite governing only wholesale participation. To aggregate a distributed battery into a wholesale-market bid, an RTO must be able to verify, resource by resource, that the aggregator's claimed output is real; that verification depends on telemetry equipment the resource's owner typically installs at the same time as the interconnection equipment itself, on the same electrical panel a distribution utility is already reviewing. In practice, this means the same interconnection application a state utility processes for retail purposes often carries, bundled alongside it, the equipment a customer will later use for wholesale aggregation, even though the two review processes remain legally and procedurally separate. A distribution utility reviewing that application has no obligation under Federal Order 2222-B to expedite it, and most report that they do not distinguish applications bound for future aggregation from any other application in their queue.

That non-distinction is itself a policy choice a state could revisit, though this report takes no position on whether it should; noting the choice exists is enough for the policy landscape this section is meant to establish. What the choice does illustrate is the boundary this report holds to throughout: the federal government, through Federal Order 2222-B, sets the rules for wholesale-market participation once a resource is already connected and verified; each state, through its own distribution utilities and its own statutes, retains full authority over the retail interconnection process itself, including the queue mechanics, technical standards, and cost-allocation questions Sections 3 through 6 take up in turn. Model legislation discussed later in this report is model state legislation for exactly that reason: nothing proposed here requires, or would even be capable of producing, a change to Federal Order 2222-B or to any RTO's wholesale-market rules.

A state legislature that wants to shorten its own interconnection queue does not need to wait for, or lobby for, any federal action at all; the authority to act already sits with the state, in the same statutes and utility commission rules that created the current queue in the first place. That is, in one sense, good news: the reforms this report describes are within reach of a single state acting alone, on a timeline that single state controls, rather than depending on a federal proceeding whose timeline no state controls. It is also, in another sense, the reason the variation documented in Section 1 exists at all. Because retail interconnection authority sits entirely with the states, and because Federal Order 2222-B does nothing to standardize it, eighteen states surveyed in Section 1 arrived at eighteen different versions of a fast-track threshold, a supplemental-review trigger, and a deposit rule, each shaped by that state's own utility commission proceedings rather than by any shared federal baseline. Section 3 begins with the mechanics those state-level choices actually govern, starting with how an application moves through the queue stage by stage, from initial submission through the fast-track screen to the supplemental review Section 1 identified as the largest single source of delay, and where each of those stages sits within a distribution utility's own process rather than any federal one.
