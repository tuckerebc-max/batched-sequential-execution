## 1. Problem Statement

A homeowner or small business that wants to add a battery energy storage system (BESS) to an existing distributed solar array must first clear a process most people outside the utility sector have never heard of: interconnection review. Before a BESS can legally connect to the distribution grid and begin charging or discharging, the local distribution utility has to confirm the equipment is safe, that it will not overload nearby circuits, and, if it might, that someone has agreed to pay for the upgrade that fixes that. This report is about how long that confirmation takes, why it varies so widely from state to state, and what a state legislature or energy office can actually change about it.

The scale of the variation is the first thing worth sitting with. A 2026 eighteen-state survey found a median time from application to permission-to-operate of 61 days [S2] for a residential BESS under 25 kW, with a range across responding states of 12 to 190 days [S2]. For a commercial BESS between 25 kW and 1 MW, the median stretches to 148 days [S2], with a reported range of 40 to 310 days [S2]. A homeowner in one state can be generating and storing power within two weeks of applying; a commercial applicant in another state can wait the better part of a year before the same permission arrives. Nothing about the underlying technology explains that gap. The same battery, installed by the same contractor, would clear review in radically different amounts of time depending only on which distribution utility's queue it happened to land in, and the survey data gives no evidence that the slower states are reviewing more carefully rather than simply more slowly.

Utility-reported data narrows the explanation somewhat without fully closing it. Across four utilities that publish queue dashboards, active queue size ranged from 640 applications to 4,780 applications [S3], and one utility's queue grew 34 percent [S3] between Q4 2024 and Q4 2025 on its own account. All four utilities report that supplemental review, the deeper engineering study triggered when an application might require a distribution system upgrade, adds a median of 90 additional days [S3] regardless of system size, and that supplemental review is the single largest driver of the variance in queue time across applications [S3]. So part of the story is simply volume: more applications, especially more commercial applications sized to trigger supplemental review, sitting in the same engineering review pipeline that existed before batteries arrived at this scale, competing for the same limited engineering staff hours the utility already had before demand grew.

But volume alone does not explain why fourteen of eighteen surveyed states [S2] still process battery applications through a fast-track process designed around the equipment and system sizes typical in 2013, while only six of eighteen [S2] have updated the fast-track size threshold since 2020 to reflect how much larger a typical residential battery has become in the years since. A process built for a smaller, more homogeneous fleet of equipment routes a growing share of today's applications into the slower, discretionary review track by default, not because those applications are unusually risky, but because the threshold that would sort them into the fast track has simply not moved to match the equipment now being installed. Nine of eighteen surveyed states [S2] also require a queue-position deposit, and five of those nine [S2] report that the deposit becomes non-refundable once a utility has begun supplemental review, even if the customer withdraws before it finishes, a rule several state staff described as discouraging speculative applications but also, by their own account, harder on lower-income applicants who cannot absorb a forfeited deposit if a project falls through for reasons unrelated to the application's merits.

Two policy questions follow from this, though neither is answered in this section. First, procedural design: whether fast-track thresholds, supplemental-review triggers, and deposit rules can be adjusted to move more of today's routine battery applications out of discretionary review without weakening the review that genuinely risky applications still need. Second, cost allocation: whether the cost of the distribution upgrades that supplemental review sometimes finds necessary should fall on the individual applicant who happened to trigger the threshold, on ratepayers generally, or on some hybrid of the two, and whether that choice changes depending on how large the utility's existing queue already is. Section 2 places these two questions in the context of what Federal Order 2222-B does and does not change about a state's own authority to answer them; Sections 3 through 6 take up the mechanics of the queue itself, the cost-allocation options a state could choose among, two states' composite attempts at reform, and the risks each stakeholder group would raise about acting at all.

What the survey data does make clear, respondent by respondent, is where the eighteen states themselves believe the bottleneck sits. Eleven of eighteen respondents [S2] named utility engineering capacity, not the design of the policy itself, as the most commonly cited constraint on faster interconnection, ahead of statutory deadlines, deposit rules, or any other procedural feature this section has described. Whether that is a capacity problem policy can fix, or a capacity problem that persists regardless of what a legislature changes, is the question the rest of this report tries to answer one piece at a time, starting with how the queue itself actually works.

It is worth being precise about what this report is not claiming. It is not claiming that regional transmission organization rules are the source of retail delay; a regional transmission organization governs wholesale-market participation once a resource is already interconnected, a distinct question this report does not take up (Section 2 draws that line explicitly). It is also not claiming that twelve states with no statutory decision deadline at all, and six more with a deadline but no penalty for missing it [S2], are acting in bad faith; several of the same respondents that named engineering capacity as the binding constraint also said a statutory deadline without added engineering staff would simply shift where the delay shows up on the calendar, not remove it. The report takes that possibility seriously rather than assuming a faster-sounding rule is automatically a better one, which is precisely why Sections 4 and 5 spend as much space on tradeoffs as on results.
