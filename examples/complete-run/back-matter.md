## Conclusion and Recommendation

RTA should adopt an off-peak fare cap, and it should do so through Option B:
a corridor-first pilot in the four corridors — Eastside, Harbor, Millbrook,
and Southgate — where reduced-fare enrollment concentrates most heavily,
rather than a system-wide launch or an enrollment-only product.

The case for adopting a cap at all rests on evidence already stated in full
above: three peer agencies each grew off-peak ridership without a
full-fare-equivalent revenue loss (Section 1); RTA's own off-peak boardings
already run at RTA's lowest revenue per boarding and its lowest seat
occupancy, meaning there is room to carry more off-peak riders without added
service (Section 2); and RTA's reduced-fare riders — proportionally twice as
likely as the ridership base overall to be traveling off-peak — are exactly
the population the current all-day cap was never designed to reach (Section
3). Doing nothing leaves that population permanently outside the cap's
benefit.

The case for Option B specifically, rather than Option A's system-wide
launch or Option C's enrollment-only design, is a case about sequencing and
who it reaches, not about whether the underlying idea works. Option C would
build the cheapest product, but by the equity office's own account (Section
3) it would leave every unenrolled-but-eligible low-income rider exactly as
unreached as today's all-day cap leaves them — a real population whose size
RTA has not yet measured. Option A would reach every rider at once, but it
commits RTA to the full $410,000, nine-month real-time technology investment
(Section 4) before RTA has a single data point of its own on how its riders
respond, relying entirely on three other agencies' networks instead. Option
B avoids both problems: it reaches the population Section 3 identifies as
most underserved, and it can launch on the lower-cost, faster interim
technology path (Section 4) to generate RTA-specific revenue data before the
Board commits to the larger real-time investment system-wide — precisely
the sequencing RTA's own Finance office described as the fiscally
disciplined way to proceed (Section 5).

Adopting Option B does not mean adopting Ashford Regional Transit's pilot
uncritically. Ashford's own after-action report is explicit that its
corridor-only awareness campaign reached only a third of surveyed riders by
month six (Section 1), and RTA's pilot should not repeat that result: a
dedicated awareness effort in the four target corridors needs to be built
into the pilot's launch, not treated as an afterthought once the technology
change ships (Section 5). Two further conditions from the stakeholder record
(Section 5) apply regardless of which option the Board ultimately chooses:
RTA's driver union is owed at least 60 days' advance notice before any fare
change takes effect on vehicles, and RTA Access requires its own separate
board action before any change extends to paratransit fares — Access riders
are not, on their own, a target population of this proposal.

None of this resolves every open question. The Equity office has not sized
the population eligible for reduced fare but not yet enrolled in it, and any
enrollment-adjacent design, including a corridor-first pilot, should track
that gap rather than assume it away. But on the evidence collected here, a
corridor-first, interim-technology-first pilot in RTA's four highest-need
corridors is the option that reaches the riders the current cap most
clearly fails, without committing RTA's full technology budget before RTA
knows, from its own network, that the investment is worth it.
