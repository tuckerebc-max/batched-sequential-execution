## 3. Equity and Access Implications

RTA's day-part data shows an off-peak segment that is smaller than daytime
or AM peak but growing fastest; RTA's Equity & Access office analysis
answers who is actually in that segment. Enrolled reduced-fare riders — a
category limited to riders documented in one of four qualifying
income-based assistance programs — account for 19% of RTA's system-wide
boardings but 33% of off-peak boardings [S4]. Proportionally, a reduced-fare
rider is roughly twice as likely to be traveling off-peak as the ridership
base overall [S4]. RTA has 41,000 riders enrolled in the reduced-fare
program as of FY2026 [S4].

The Equity office's working explanation, drawn from a FY2025 rider survey,
is that enrolled riders disproportionately work evening, overnight, or
weekend shifts in retail, food service, and healthcare support roles, and
disproportionately make non-work trips — grocery, medical, childcare
pickup — outside the traditional nine-to-five travel window the current
all-day cap was designed around [S4]. That design point matters here
because of how the all-day cap actually pays off: a rider who taps twice
during the morning commute reaches the cap on the commute alone and then
rides free for any trip later that day, including an evening or weekend
trip. A rider whose only trips are off-peak — the pattern the Equity
office's survey associates most closely with the reduced-fare population —
never reaches the cap on those trips and pays full fare for both taps [S4].
The Equity office states this plainly: the current fare structure's largest
cap benefit accrues to peak commuters, not to the reduced-fare population
the program exists to serve [S4]. Put another way, RTA's all-day cap was
never designed with time of day in mind, but the population it happens to
reward best — riders who tap twice inside a single peak commute — and the
population the Equity office's survey associates most with off-peak-only
travel are, for a meaningful share of enrolled riders, two different groups
of the same ridership base.

Reduced-fare enrollment is also not spread evenly across RTA's network. It
concentrates in four of RTA's twelve service corridors — Eastside, Harbor,
Millbrook, and Southgate — which together hold 58% of all enrolled riders
while carrying only 31% of system-wide boardings [S4]. All four corridors
also show above-average off-peak ridership growth over the past two years,
though the Equity office is explicit that this overlap is suggestive, not
proven: it has not run a corridor-level analysis isolating how much of each
corridor's off-peak growth is attributable to the reduced-fare population
specifically, as opposed to other riders in the same corridors [S4]. The
practical implication, whatever the eventual regression shows, is that any
implementation option built around geography rather than enrollment status
would land disproportionately on exactly these four corridors, simply
because that is where enrolled ridership already concentrates.

The Equity office also produced a preliminary estimate of what an off-peak
cap could mean for this population, by applying Meridian Transit's reported
11% off-peak ridership response to RTA's four highest-concentration
corridors: on that basis, enrolled riders there could see an off-peak cost
reduction comparable to what Cedar Falls Regional Transit reported for its
own enrolled population [S4]. The office is explicit that this is an
extrapolation from two peer agencies' results, not a Riverton-specific
model built from RTA's own ridership response [S4].

One caution the Equity office asked to be carried forward into any proposal:
evaluate whether it also serves low-income riders who have not completed
reduced-fare enrollment, since a cap benefit tied only to enrollment status
would miss a share of the population the equity goal is meant to reach [S4].
The office did not estimate how large that unenrolled-but-eligible group is
[S4], which leaves open, for whatever implementation option RTA considers,
whether enrollment status alone is the right way to target a new cap or
whether outreach to unenrolled-eligible riders needs to be part of the same
effort — a distinction any option RTA later adopts will need to address
explicitly rather than assume away. Whether RTA's fare system can even support a cap that behaves
differently by time of day, separately from who it reaches, is a question
of technical capability the next section addresses.
