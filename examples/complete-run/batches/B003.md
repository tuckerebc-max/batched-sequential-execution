## 2. RTA Ridership and Revenue by Time of Day

RTA recorded 21.4 million boardings systemwide in FY2026, up 1.8% from
FY2025, and $28.6 million in fixed-route fare revenue [S2]. RTA has run a
single fare product since 2019 [S2]: an all-day cap under which a rider
pays full fare for their first two taps in a service day, 6:00 a.m.-11:59
p.m., and rides free the rest of that day no matter when those first two
taps happen [S2]. That design mechanically rewards a specific travel
pattern. A rider who taps twice during the morning commute reaches the cap
on the commute alone and then rides free for any trip later that day,
including an evening or weekend trip; a rider whose only trips fall outside
the morning commute never reaches the cap on those trips and pays full fare
for each one [S2]. The cap, in other words, is time-blind by design but not
time-blind in effect: it pays off fastest for a peak-anchored, multi-trip
rider and offers nothing extra to a rider whose travel is concentrated later
in the day, however many separate trips that rider makes.

This matters against the peer-agency findings in Section 1. Meridian and
Cedar Falls both built their off-peak cap on top of an existing account-based
fare product much like RiverPass; RTA's own all-day cap is exactly that kind
of product, already in place and already capable of being read at the point
of a tap. The open question Section 1 could not answer on its own is
whether RTA's riders are actually distributed across the day in a way that
would make a second, off-peak-specific cap worth building. The day-part data
below answers that question directly.

RTA's own fare-collection system divides each service day into three
day-parts for reporting: AM peak (6:00-9:30 a.m.), daytime (9:30 a.m.-6:30
p.m., which folds in the PM peak, 4:00-6:30 p.m.), and off-peak (6:30
p.m.-11:59 p.m., plus all weekend hours) [S2]. In FY2026, AM peak
accounted for 6.2 million boardings, 29% of the system total, and $9.1
million in revenue [S2]. Daytime accounted for 10.5 million boardings, 49%
of the total, and $13.8 million in revenue [S2]. Off-peak accounted for 4.7
million boardings, 22% of the total, and $5.7 million in revenue [S2].
Average revenue per boarding is highest in AM peak, at $1.47, and lowest
off-peak, at $1.21 [S2] — a gap the all-day cap's mechanics explain directly:
RTA Planning estimates 38% of off-peak boardings are made by riders who had
already reached the cap earlier the same day and are riding for no
incremental revenue at all, while the remaining 62% are riders making an
off-peak-only trip, or a second trip on a day when their first trip was also
off-peak, and so paying full fare with no cap benefit either way [S2].

Off-peak ridership is also RTA's fastest-growing segment: it rose 3.1% year
over year, against 1.8% growth system-wide, driven mostly by evening service
on RTA's two light-rail lines [S2]. That growth has not run into a capacity
constraint. RTA's fleet-utilization data shows off-peak trips running at 34%
average seat occupancy system-wide, compared with 78% in AM peak and 71% in
PM peak, and no route in the FY2026 schedule is capacity-constrained during
off-peak hours [S2]. Whatever ridership response an off-peak-specific cap
produced, RTA's existing off-peak service already has the seats to carry it
without adding trips or altering the current evening and weekend schedule
RTA already runs.

RTA has changed its fare structure twice in recent history: the current
all-day cap was introduced in 2019, and the base fare itself was last
increased by $0.15 in 2021 [S2]. Both changes went through a 90-day public
comment period under RTA's fare-change policy, and neither introduced a
product differentiated by time of day — the all-day cap has applied
uniformly regardless of when a rider's first two taps occur since it began
[S2]. RTA has not previously operated a fare product that treats an evening
or weekend trip differently from a morning or midday one, which is the gap
an off-peak-specific cap would be the first RTA product to close. Whether
that gap matters most for revenue, for ridership, or for which riders it
reaches is a question the day-part figures above only partly answer, since
a boardings-and-revenue breakdown by time of day says nothing on its own
about which riders make up each of those three day-parts or why. The
equity implications of who is actually riding in each of these day-parts,
and how well RTA's current all-day cap serves them, are taken up next.
