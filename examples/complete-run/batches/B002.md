## 1. How Off-Peak Fare Capping Works and What Peer Agencies Found

An off-peak fare cap is a fare product that pays off after fewer taps than a
system's ordinary cap, but only for taps that fall inside a defined
off-peak window — typically evenings, nights, and weekends. That design
differs from a flat, time-blind cap of the kind RTA already runs, which pays
off after a set number of taps regardless of when in the day those taps
occur. Three peer agencies comparable to RTA in size and service area have
implemented an off-peak fare cap and reported what happened. None of their
figures below have been independently audited by RTA; they are as each
agency reported them.

Meridian Transit, serving a population of roughly 310,000, launched a
system-wide off-peak fare cap in March 2023 [S1]. Riders who tap six times
between 9:00 a.m. and 3:00 p.m., or after 7:00 p.m., ride free for the rest
of that service day; peak-period riders still need twelve taps to reach the
cap [S1]. In the twelve months after launch, Meridian's off-peak boardings
rose 11%, against 2% system-wide growth over the same window [S1]. Off-peak
revenue per boarding fell 9%, but total off-peak revenue still rose 4% on
the higher volume [S1]. Meridian's finance office attributes most of the
ridership gain to riders who previously skipped a second midday trip once
they had already paid a peak fare that morning [S1]. The change cost
$340,000 in fare-system software and took 7 months from board approval to
launch, applied automatically through Meridian's existing account-based fare
system with no change to fare enforcement [S1].

Cedar Falls Regional Transit, serving about 265,000 people, took a narrower
approach, launching its off-peak cap in September 2022 restricted to riders
already enrolled in its reduced-fare program [S1]. Enrolled off-peak riders
pay a flat $0.75 per boarding after their second off-peak tap in a day, down
from a $1.25 per-boarding fare [S1]. Cedar Falls reports enrolled riders'
off-peak boardings rose 18% in the first year, and cites exit-survey data
showing 61% of enrolled riders who increased off-peak travel named the cap
as the reason [S1]. Because the program reached only enrolled riders,
system-wide fare revenue fell just 0.6% [S1]. Its technology cost was lower,
$95,000, since the cap only had to be evaluated against one rider class
already flagged in the fare system [S1] — but Cedar Falls' own board minutes
name the tradeoff directly: a low-income rider who has not completed
reduced-fare enrollment sees no benefit at all [S1].

Ashford Regional Transit, serving roughly 220,000 people, tested its cap
differently again: a 14-month pilot from March 2024 through May 2025 on a
single high-ridership corridor, capping off-peak boardings at four taps a
day on that corridor only [S1]. Corridor off-peak ridership rose 7% — a
smaller gain than Meridian's system-wide result, which Ashford's own
after-action report attributes to the pilot's narrow scope: only 34% of
surveyed riders on the corridor knew the cap existed by month six [S1]. The
pilot cost $110,000 and took 5 months to stand up, reusing existing
fare-system logic rather than building new account-based capping [S1].
Ashford's board voted in June 2025 to expand the cap system-wide starting in
2027, judging the ridership gain sufficient evidence despite the low
awareness rate [S1].

Read together, the three agencies' results point in the same direction from
different starting positions. All three report an off-peak ridership gain
without a full-fare-equivalent revenue loss, because the riders who
responded to each cap were largely riders who would otherwise have made zero
or one off-peak trip, not riders substituting away from a peak fare they had
already paid [S1]. None of the three reported a measurable change in vehicle
crowding or added service cost from the extra off-peak ridership; all three
had spare off-peak capacity before launch [S1]. Meridian and Cedar Falls
both built on an existing account-based fare system with a software change;
Ashford's corridor-only approach cost and took less to build, but its own
board later judged it an insufficient basis for a system-wide case without
the awareness problem being solved first [S1]. None of the three agencies
reported a labor-relations dispute tied to the fare change itself, though
Meridian's operator union did request, and receive, six weeks of advance
notice before the change took effect on vehicles [S1]. What these three
outcomes would mean applied to RTA's own network — rather than Meridian's,
Cedar Falls', or Ashford's — depends on how RTA's own riders are distributed
across the day, which is the subject the next section takes up.
