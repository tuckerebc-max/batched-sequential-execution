# Equity office analysis notes — off-peak ridership and low-income riders

Prepared by RTA's Equity & Access office, 2026-08, using FY2026 reduced-fare
program enrollment as a proxy for low-income ridership (RTA does not collect
rider income directly; reduced-fare enrollment requires documented enrollment
in one of four qualifying income-based assistance programs).

## Enrollment and travel pattern

RTA's reduced-fare program has 41,000 enrolled riders as of FY2026. Enrolled
riders account for 19% of system-wide boardings but 33% of off-peak
boardings — reduced-fare riders are roughly twice as likely, proportionally,
to be traveling off-peak as the ridership base overall. The Equity office's
working explanation, drawn from a FY2025 rider survey, is that enrolled
riders disproportionately work evening, overnight, or weekend shifts in
retail, food service, and healthcare support roles, and disproportionately
make non-work trips (grocery, medical, childcare pickup) outside the
traditional 9-to-5 travel window that the current all-day cap was designed
around.

## Why the current all-day cap underserves this group

Because RTA's existing all-day cap only pays off once a rider has already
taken 2 taps that day, it mechanically benefits multi-trip peak-anchored
travel most: a rider who commutes in and out during peak hours reaches the
cap on their commute alone and then rides free the rest of the day, including
any off-peak trips. A rider whose only trips that day are off-peak — the
pattern the Equity office's survey associates most with enrolled reduced-fare
riders — pays full fare for both taps and never reaches the cap at all. The
Equity office's analysis states plainly that the current fare structure's
largest cap benefit accrues to peak commuters, not to the reduced-fare
population the program exists to serve.

## Geographic concentration

Reduced-fare enrollment is concentrated in four of RTA's twelve service
corridors (the Eastside, Harbor, Millbrook, and Southgate corridors), which
together account for 58% of all enrolled riders despite carrying 31% of
system-wide boardings. All four of these corridors also appear in RTA's
off-peak ridership growth data as above-average growth corridors over the
past two years. The Equity office notes this overlap as suggestive, not
proven: it has not run a corridor-level regression isolating the reduced-fare
population's contribution to each corridor's off-peak growth.

## What an off-peak cap could change

An off-peak-specific cap — one that pays off after fewer taps than the
current all-day cap, but only for taps in the off-peak window — would create
a path to fare relief for a rider whose only travel that day is off-peak,
which the current structure does not. The Equity office's preliminary
estimate, based on applying Meridian Transit's reported 11% off-peak
ridership response to RTA's reduced-fare-heavy corridors only, is that
enrolled riders in the four highest-concentration corridors could see
off-peak trip costs fall by an amount comparable to Cedar Falls Regional
Transit's reported outcome for its enrolled population, though the Equity
office is explicit that this is an extrapolation from two peer agencies, not
a Riverton-specific model.

## Caution the office asked to be carried forward

The Equity office's notes ask explicitly that any proposal be evaluated
against whether it also serves non-enrolled low-income riders — people who
would qualify for reduced fare but have not completed enrollment — since a
cap benefit tied only to enrollment status would miss a share of the
population the equity goal is meant to reach; the office did not estimate
the size of that unenrolled-but-eligible group in this analysis.
