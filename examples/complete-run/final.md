# Off-Peak Fare Capping at Riverton Transit Authority: A Briefing Note

## Executive Summary

Riverton Transit Authority (RTA) has run a single fare product since 2019: an
all-day cap that pays off after a rider's first two taps in a service day,
regardless of when those taps occur. That design rewards riders who commute
during the morning peak and then ride free the rest of the day; it does
nothing for a rider whose only trips are in the evening, at night, or on
weekends. This briefing asks whether RTA should add a second product — an
off-peak fare cap that pays off after fewer taps, but only for taps inside
the off-peak window — and, if so, how to roll one out.

The evidence points toward yes, phased rather than all at once. Three peer
agencies that added an off-peak cap each reported an off-peak ridership gain
without a full-fare-equivalent revenue loss, because the riders who
responded were largely riders making a trip they otherwise would not have
made, not riders substituting away from a peak fare they had already paid.
RTA's own data shows why that pattern would likely repeat here: 22% of RTA's
boardings happen off-peak, at RTA's lowest average revenue per boarding, on
routes running at less than half the seat occupancy of the morning peak.
RTA's Equity & Access office's analysis sharpens who stands to gain most: RTA's
reduced-fare riders are proportionally twice as likely to be traveling
off-peak as the ridership base overall, and the current all-day cap
structurally excludes exactly this group from any cap benefit at all.

Building a cap that treats time of day differently is not free. RTA's fare
system currently evaluates the existing cap overnight, not in real time, and
a genuine time-of-day product needs that evaluation moved to the moment of
the tap — a $410,000, nine-month change, against a lower-cost, lower-fidelity
$85,000, three-month alternative that settles the difference as an account
credit days later rather than at the farebox. Three stakeholders have
already weighed in: the driver union wants substantial advance notice before
any change reaches vehicles, RTA Access needs a separate board action before
anything extends to paratransit fares, and RTA's own Finance office favors
testing the revenue assumption on a smaller base before committing to the
larger technology spend.

Sections 1 through 5 lay out this evidence in full: what peer agencies
found, what RTA's own ridership and revenue data shows, who the current cap
underserves, what building a time-of-day cap would actually take, and three
concrete phasing options with their tradeoffs. The Conclusion states this
briefing's recommendation once that evidence is in front of the reader; no
section before it does.

## 1. How the Off-Peak Fare Cap Works and What Peer Agencies Found

An off-peak fare cap is a fare product that pays off after fewer taps than a
system's ordinary cap, but only for taps that fall inside a defined
off-peak window — typically evenings, nights, and weekends. That design
differs from a flat, time-blind cap of the kind RTA already runs, which pays
off after a set number of taps regardless of when in the day those taps
occur. Three peer agencies comparable to RTA in size and service area have
implemented an off-peak fare cap and reported what happened. None of their
figures below have been independently audited by RTA; they are as each
agency reported them.

Meridian Transit, serving a population of roughly 310,000, launched a
system-wide off-peak fare cap in March 2023 [S1]. Riders who tap six times
between 9:00 a.m. and 3:00 p.m., or after 7:00 p.m., ride free for the rest
of that service day; peak-period riders still need twelve taps to reach the
cap [S1]. In the twelve months after launch, Meridian's off-peak boardings
rose 11%, against 2% system-wide growth over the same window [S1]. Off-peak
revenue per boarding fell 9%, but total off-peak revenue still rose 4% on
the higher volume [S1]. Meridian's finance office attributes most of the
ridership gain to riders who previously skipped a second midday trip once
they had already paid a peak fare that morning [S1]. The change cost
$340,000 in fare-system software and took 7 months from board approval to
launch, applied automatically through Meridian's existing account-based fare
system with no change to fare enforcement [S1].

Cedar Falls Regional Transit, serving about 265,000 people, took a narrower
approach, launching its off-peak cap in September 2022 restricted to riders
already enrolled in its reduced-fare program [S1]. Enrolled off-peak riders
pay a flat $0.75 per boarding after their second off-peak tap in a day, down
from a $1.25 per-boarding fare [S1]. Cedar Falls reports enrolled riders'
off-peak boardings rose 18% in the first year, and cites exit-survey data
showing 61% of enrolled riders who increased off-peak travel named the cap
as the reason [S1]. Because the program reached only enrolled riders,
system-wide fare revenue fell just 0.6% [S1]. Its technology cost was lower,
$95,000, since the cap only had to be evaluated against one rider class
already flagged in the fare system [S1] — but Cedar Falls' own board minutes
name the tradeoff directly: a low-income rider who has not completed
reduced-fare enrollment sees no benefit at all [S1].

Ashford Regional Transit, serving roughly 220,000 people, tested its cap
differently again: a 14-month pilot from March 2024 through May 2025 on a
single high-ridership corridor, capping off-peak boardings at four taps a
day on that corridor only [S1]. Corridor off-peak ridership rose 7% — a
smaller gain than Meridian's system-wide result, which Ashford's own
after-action report attributes to the pilot's narrow scope: only 34% of
surveyed riders on the corridor knew the cap existed by month six [S1]. The
pilot cost $110,000 and took 5 months to stand up, reusing existing
fare-system logic rather than building new account-based capping [S1].
Ashford's board voted in June 2025 to expand the cap system-wide starting in
2027, judging the ridership gain sufficient evidence despite the low
awareness rate [S1].

Read together, the three agencies' results point in the same direction from
different starting positions. All three report an off-peak ridership gain
without a full-fare-equivalent revenue loss, because the riders who
responded to each cap were largely riders who would otherwise have made zero
or one off-peak trip, not riders substituting away from a peak fare they had
already paid [S1]. None of the three reported a measurable change in vehicle
crowding or added service cost from the extra off-peak ridership; all three
had spare off-peak capacity before launch [S1]. Meridian and Cedar Falls
both built on an existing account-based fare system with a software change;
Ashford's corridor-only approach cost and took less to build, but its own
board later judged it an insufficient basis for a system-wide case without
the awareness problem being solved first [S1]. None of the three agencies
reported a labor-relations dispute tied to the fare change itself, though
Meridian's operator union did request, and receive, six weeks of advance
notice before the change took effect on vehicles [S1]. What these three
outcomes would mean applied to RTA's own network — rather than Meridian's,
Cedar Falls', or Ashford's — depends on how RTA's own riders are distributed
across the day, which is the subject the next section takes up.

## 2. RTA Ridership and Revenue by Time of Day

RTA recorded 21.4 million boardings systemwide in FY2026, up 1.8% from
FY2025, and $28.6 million in fixed-route fare revenue [S2]. RTA has run a
single fare product since 2019 [S2]: an all-day cap under which a rider
pays full fare for their first two taps in a service day, 6:00 a.m.-11:59
p.m., and rides free the rest of that day no matter when those first two
taps happen [S2]. That design mechanically rewards a specific travel
pattern. A rider who taps twice during the morning commute reaches the cap
on the commute alone and then rides free for any trip later that day,
including an evening or weekend trip; a rider whose only trips fall outside
the morning commute never reaches the cap on those trips and pays full fare
for each one [S2]. The cap, in other words, is time-blind by design but not
time-blind in effect: it pays off fastest for a peak-anchored, multi-trip
rider and offers nothing extra to a rider whose travel is concentrated later
in the day, however many separate trips that rider makes.

This matters against the peer-agency findings in Section 1. Meridian and
Cedar Falls both built their off-peak cap on top of an existing account-based
fare product much like RiverPass; RTA's own all-day cap is exactly that kind
of product, already in place and already capable of being read at the point
of a tap. The open question Section 1 could not answer on its own is
whether RTA's riders are actually distributed across the day in a way that
would make a second, off-peak-specific cap worth building. The day-part data
below answers that question directly.

RTA's own fare-collection system divides each service day into three
day-parts for reporting: AM peak (6:00-9:30 a.m.), daytime (9:30 a.m.-6:30
p.m., which folds in the PM peak, 4:00-6:30 p.m.), and off-peak (6:30
p.m.-11:59 p.m., plus all weekend hours) [S2]. In FY2026, AM peak
accounted for 6.2 million boardings, 29% of the system total, and $9.1
million in revenue [S2]. Daytime accounted for 10.5 million boardings, 49%
of the total, and $13.8 million in revenue [S2]. Off-peak accounted for 4.7
million boardings, 22% of the total, and $5.7 million in revenue [S2].
Average revenue per boarding is highest in AM peak, at $1.47, and lowest
off-peak, at $1.21 [S2] — a gap the all-day cap's mechanics explain directly:
RTA Planning estimates 38% of off-peak boardings are made by riders who had
already reached the cap earlier the same day and are riding for no
incremental revenue at all, while the remaining 62% are riders making an
off-peak-only trip, or a second trip on a day when their first trip was also
off-peak, and so paying full fare with no cap benefit either way [S2].

Off-peak ridership is also RTA's fastest-growing segment: it rose 3.1% year
over year, against 1.8% growth system-wide, driven mostly by evening service
on RTA's two light-rail lines [S2]. That growth has not run into a capacity
constraint. RTA's fleet-utilization data shows off-peak trips running at 34%
average seat occupancy system-wide, compared with 78% in AM peak and 71% in
PM peak, and no route in the FY2026 schedule is capacity-constrained during
off-peak hours [S2]. Whatever ridership response an off-peak-specific cap
produced, RTA's existing off-peak service already has the seats to carry it
without adding trips or altering the current evening and weekend schedule
RTA already runs.

RTA has changed its fare structure twice in recent history: the current
all-day cap was introduced in 2019, and the base fare itself was last
increased by $0.15 in 2021 [S2]. Both changes went through a 90-day public
comment period under RTA's fare-change policy, and neither introduced a
product differentiated by time of day — the all-day cap has applied
uniformly regardless of when a rider's first two taps occur since it began
[S2]. RTA has not previously operated a fare product that treats an evening
or weekend trip differently from a morning or midday one, which is the gap
an off-peak-specific cap would be the first RTA product to close. Whether
that gap matters most for revenue, for ridership, or for which riders it
reaches is a question the day-part figures above only partly answer, since
a boardings-and-revenue breakdown by time of day says nothing on its own
about which riders make up each of those three day-parts or why. The
equity implications of who is actually riding in each of these day-parts,
and how well RTA's current all-day cap serves them, are taken up next.

## 3. Equity and Access Implications

RTA's day-part data shows an off-peak segment that is smaller than daytime
or AM peak but growing fastest; RTA's Equity & Access office analysis
answers who is actually in that segment. Enrolled reduced-fare riders — a
category limited to riders documented in one of four qualifying
income-based assistance programs — account for 19% of RTA's system-wide
boardings but 33% of off-peak boardings [S4]. Proportionally, a reduced-fare
rider is roughly twice as likely to be traveling off-peak as the ridership
base overall [S4]. RTA has 41,000 riders enrolled in the reduced-fare
program as of FY2026 [S4].

The Equity office's working explanation, drawn from a FY2025 rider survey,
is that enrolled riders disproportionately work evening, overnight, or
weekend shifts in retail, food service, and healthcare support roles, and
disproportionately make non-work trips — grocery, medical, childcare
pickup — outside the traditional nine-to-five travel window the current
all-day cap was designed around [S4]. That design point matters here because
of how the all-day cap actually pays off, as Section 2 already established:
it rewards a peak-anchored commute, not a day made up only of off-peak
trips. A rider whose only trips are off-peak — the pattern the Equity
office's survey associates most closely with the reduced-fare population —
never reaches the cap on those trips and pays full fare for both taps [S4].
The Equity office states this plainly: the current fare structure's largest
cap benefit accrues to peak commuters, not to the reduced-fare population
the program exists to serve [S4]. Put another way, RTA's all-day cap was
never designed with time of day in mind, but the population it happens to
reward best — riders who tap twice inside a single peak commute — and the
population the Equity office's survey associates most with off-peak-only
travel are, for a meaningful share of enrolled riders, two different groups
of the same ridership base.

Reduced-fare enrollment is also not spread evenly across RTA's network. It
concentrates in four of RTA's twelve service corridors — Eastside, Harbor,
Millbrook, and Southgate — which together hold 58% of all enrolled riders
while carrying only 31% of system-wide boardings [S4]. All four corridors
also show above-average off-peak ridership growth over the past two years,
though the Equity office is explicit that this overlap is suggestive, not
proven: it has not run a corridor-level analysis isolating how much of each
corridor's off-peak growth is attributable to the reduced-fare population
specifically, as opposed to other riders in the same corridors [S4]. The
practical implication, whatever the eventual regression shows, is that any
implementation option built around geography rather than enrollment status
would land disproportionately on exactly these four corridors, simply
because that is where enrolled ridership already concentrates.

The Equity office also produced a preliminary estimate of what an off-peak
cap could mean for this population, by applying Meridian Transit's reported
11% off-peak ridership response to RTA's four highest-concentration
corridors: on that basis, enrolled riders there could see an off-peak cost
reduction comparable to what Cedar Falls Regional Transit reported for its
own enrolled population [S4]. The office is explicit that this is an
extrapolation from two peer agencies' results, not a Riverton-specific
model built from RTA's own ridership response [S4].

One caution the Equity office asked to be carried forward into any proposal:
evaluate whether it also serves low-income riders who have not completed
reduced-fare enrollment, since a cap benefit tied only to enrollment status
would miss a share of the population the equity goal is meant to reach [S4].
The office did not estimate how large that unenrolled-but-eligible group is
[S4], which leaves open, for whatever implementation option RTA considers,
whether enrollment status alone is the right way to target a new cap or
whether outreach to unenrolled-eligible riders needs to be part of the same
effort — a distinction any option RTA later adopts will need to address
explicitly rather than assume away. Whether RTA's fare system can even
support a cap that behaves differently by time of day, separately from who
it reaches, is a question of technical capability the next section
addresses.

## 4. Operational and Technical Feasibility

RTA's fare system, RiverPass, validates a tap in real time — a rider is
authorized or declined at the farebox in under a second — but it currently
evaluates cap status, whether a rider has already reached the all-day cap,
in a nightly batch job rather than at the moment of the tap [S3]. In
practice, a rider who has already reached the cap can still be charged at
the farebox for a later tap that same day; RTA credits the overcharge back
to the rider's account the next morning once the batch job runs, which
happens for roughly 4% of all-day-cap-eligible taps in an average week
[S3].

A cap that behaves differently depending on the time of the tap cannot be
evaluated correctly by that batch process, because the batch job only
reconciles a rider's cumulative daily taps after the fact — it has no way of
knowing, at the moment a given tap happens, whether that tap falls inside or
outside the off-peak window relative to the rider's other taps that day
[S3]. Moving cap evaluation into the real-time authorization path, so the
farebox knows both the time of day and the rider's cap status at the moment
of the tap, is what a time-of-day cap requires that the all-day cap does
not.

RTA's fare-system vendor scoped two paths to get there. The first, a
real-time option, would move cap evaluation fully into the authorization
path. The vendor's quote, based on a scoping call in June 2026, breaks the
timeline into two months of vendor development, three months of RTA-side
integration testing against the existing farebox hardware, and a recommended
four-month phased rollout across RTA's roughly 340 buses and 22 light-rail
vehicles, to catch hardware-specific validation bugs before they reach the
whole fleet [S3]. The quote has not been through RTA procurement review and
is not a binding bid [S3]. The four-month phased rollout is a deliberate
choice, not a padding of the timeline: the vendor recommended it
specifically to surface hardware-specific validation bugs on a subset of
the fleet before the same software change reaches every bus and light-rail
vehicle RTA operates [S3].

The second, an interim option, would leave cap evaluation in the nightly
batch job and change only the refund rule, so a rider whose batch
reconciliation shows they qualified for an off-peak cap that day receives an
automatic same-week account credit rather than riding free at the farebox in
real time [S3]. Because it touches only the batch job's refund logic and not
the real-time authorization path, it is a smaller change. Its tradeoff, as
the vendor's scoping notes state directly, is that a rider does not
experience riding free at the point of travel — they pay full fare at the
farebox and see the credit on their account days later, which RTA's own
product staff flagged in the scoping call as a meaningfully worse rider
experience than real-time capping, even though the two paths are intended to
produce the same net fare outcome [S3].

Neither option is constrained by vehicle capacity: RTA's spare off-peak
seat capacity, already established in Section 2, means added off-peak
ridership from either option would not require added service. Nor is
farebox hardware a constraint — the hardware, installed in 2020, supports
both options without replacement, and RTA's IT department has no other
planned RiverPass changes competing for 2027 engineering capacity [S3].

What differs sharply between the two paths is cost and time to deliver. The
real-time option is quoted at $410,000 and a nine-month delivery timeline
from contract signature [S3]; the interim batch-credit option is quoted at
$85,000 and three months, because it changes only the batch job's refund
rule rather than the real-time authorization path [S3]. That is a gap of
roughly $325,000 and about six months between the two paths [S3], measured
against a rider experience that differs correspondingly: real-time capping
means a rider who has reached the cap simply is not charged again that day,
while the interim path still charges the farebox fare and settles the
difference as an account credit within the week [S3]. Which of those two
paths, or what sequencing between them, best fits RTA's own rollout is a
question of implementation choices, not fare-system capability, and is
where the next section turns.

## 5. Implementation Options and Tradeoffs

The preceding sections establish the case, the data, the population most
affected, and the technical paths available. What remains is how RTA could
sequence a rollout — a question of phasing choices, not fare-cap design,
and one where at least three distinct options are available to the Board.

**Option A: system-wide launch.** RTA could roll out an off-peak cap across
its entire network at once, on the model of Meridian Transit's approach.
This option reaches every RTA rider immediately and is the option most
directly supported by the largest peer-agency ridership result available.
It also carries the largest up-front technology commitment of the three
options, since a system-wide cap of any real value to off-peak-only riders
depends on the real-time authorization path described in Section 4, not the
interim batch-credit path — the interim path's days-later account credit
would apply to every RTA rider at once under this option, which is a
materially different rider experience to launch system-wide than to pilot
in a smaller footprint first. Because it reaches every corridor
simultaneously, Option A also gives RTA the least room to correct course if
the ridership response differs from what Meridian, Cedar Falls, or Ashford
each reported for their own networks.

**Option B: corridor-first pilot.** RTA could instead launch first in the
four corridors — Eastside, Harbor, Millbrook, and Southgate — where
reduced-fare enrollment concentrates most heavily, before any system-wide
expansion decision. This option targets the population Section 3 identifies
as most underserved by the current all-day cap without waiting for a
system-wide technology build, and could launch on the lower-cost interim
batch-credit option first, deferring the real-time investment until RTA has
its own revenue data rather than relying only on peer-agency figures. Its
clearest risk is the one Ashford Regional Transit's own after-action report
names directly: a single-corridor pilot with a corridor-only awareness
campaign reached only a third of surveyed riders within six months.
Avoiding that outcome would mean building a dedicated awareness effort into
the pilot from the start rather than treating communication as an
afterthought once the technology change ships — a sequencing choice within
Option B itself, not a separate fourth option. A corridor-first pilot would
also let RTA weigh the interim and real-time technology paths against
actual corridor-level revenue data before deciding whether the larger,
system-wide technology spend is warranted at all.

**Option C: enrollment-only cap.** RTA could instead limit an off-peak cap
to riders already enrolled in the reduced-fare program, on the model Cedar
Falls Regional Transit used. This is the narrowest and least expensive
option to build, since it evaluates cap eligibility against a rider class
RTA's fare system already flags. Its tradeoff is the one the Equity office
raised directly in Section 3: a low-income rider who has not completed
reduced-fare enrollment would see no benefit under this option, a gap
neither Option A nor Option B carries in the same way.

Three stakeholder positions bear on any of these three options. RTA's
driver union, Amalgamated Transit Operators Local 219, asked for a minimum
of 60 days' advance notice before any fare change takes effect on vehicles,
citing Meridian's six-week notice as a floor RTA's larger fleet should
exceed, not match [S5]. RTA Access, the paratransit program, made clear that
none of these three options would apply to Access fares without a separate
board action, since Access operates under its own $2.50-per-trip schedule
tied to ADA paratransit funding rules, and asked to be consulted before any
public communication implies Access riders are a target population for a
fixed-route change [S5]. RTA Finance did not endorse a specific option but
described a phased approach — testing the revenue assumption on a smaller
base before committing the larger technology spend — as "the fiscally
disciplined way" to proceed, and named Ashford's low awareness result as a
communication problem worth designing around, not a reason to avoid
phasing [S5]. Finance also asked that any board memo keep the real-time and
interim technology costs clearly distinct, since they carry materially
different procurement and cash-flow timelines [S5], regardless of which
phasing option the Board eventually favors.

None of the three options is without a real cost: Option A commits the
largest technology spend before RTA has its own revenue data; Option B adds
sequencing complexity and repeats Ashford's awareness risk unless a
communications plan is built in from the start; Option C is cheapest to
build but leaves the equity office's unenrolled-but-eligible population
exactly as unreached as the current all-day cap leaves it. Which of these
three tradeoffs the Board is prepared to accept, and in what order, is the
decision this briefing was assembled to support.

## Conclusion and Recommendation

RTA should adopt an off-peak fare cap, and it should do so through Option B:
a corridor-first pilot in the four corridors — Eastside, Harbor, Millbrook,
and Southgate — where reduced-fare enrollment concentrates most heavily,
rather than a system-wide launch or an enrollment-only product.

The case for adopting a cap at all rests on evidence already stated in full
above: three peer agencies each grew off-peak ridership without a
full-fare-equivalent revenue loss (Section 1); RTA's own off-peak boardings
already run at RTA's lowest revenue per boarding and its lowest seat
occupancy, meaning there is room to carry more off-peak riders without added
service (Section 2); and RTA's reduced-fare riders — proportionally twice as
likely as the ridership base overall to be traveling off-peak — are exactly
the population the current all-day cap was never designed to reach (Section
3). Doing nothing leaves that population permanently outside the cap's
benefit.

The case for Option B specifically, rather than Option A's system-wide
launch or Option C's enrollment-only design, is a case about sequencing and
who it reaches, not about whether the underlying idea works. Option C would
build the cheapest product, but by the equity office's own account (Section
3) it would leave every unenrolled-but-eligible low-income rider exactly as
unreached as today's all-day cap leaves them — a real population whose size
RTA has not yet measured. Option A would reach every rider at once, but it
commits RTA to the full $410,000, nine-month real-time technology investment
(Section 4) before RTA has a single data point of its own on how its riders
respond, relying entirely on three other agencies' networks instead. Option
B avoids both problems: it reaches the population Section 3 identifies as
most underserved, and it can launch on the lower-cost, faster interim
technology path (Section 4) to generate RTA-specific revenue data before the
Board commits to the larger real-time investment system-wide — precisely
the sequencing RTA's own Finance office described as the fiscally
disciplined way to proceed (Section 5).

Adopting Option B does not mean adopting Ashford Regional Transit's pilot
uncritically. Ashford's own after-action report is explicit that its
corridor-only awareness campaign reached only a third of surveyed riders by
month six (Section 1), and RTA's pilot should not repeat that result: a
dedicated awareness effort in the four target corridors needs to be built
into the pilot's launch, not treated as an afterthought once the technology
change ships (Section 5). Two further conditions from the stakeholder record
(Section 5) apply regardless of which option the Board ultimately chooses:
RTA's driver union is owed at least 60 days' advance notice before any fare
change takes effect on vehicles, and RTA Access requires its own separate
board action before any change extends to paratransit fares — Access riders
are not, on their own, a target population of this proposal.

None of this resolves every open question. The Equity office has not sized
the population eligible for reduced fare but not yet enrolled in it, and any
enrollment-adjacent design, including a corridor-first pilot, should track
that gap rather than assume it away. But on the evidence collected here, a
corridor-first, interim-technology-first pilot in RTA's four highest-need
corridors is the option that reaches the riders the current cap most
clearly fails, without committing RTA's full technology budget before RTA
knows, from its own network, that the investment is worth it.
